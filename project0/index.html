Part 1: Selfie — The Wrong Way vs. The Right Way

Instead of a human subject, I ran this experiment on my plush Labubu — the face has plenty of the same landmark features (eyes, nose/mouth, ears) that make focal-length distortion easy to see, and it holds a pose a lot better than a friend does. I kept the camera-to-subject distance and framing roughly matched to the subject's size while stepping through five focal lengths: 13mm, 24mm, 35mm, 48mm, and 100mm.

At 13mm (the wide-angle "wrong way" shot, taken close up) the center of projection sits very near the subject's face, so the nearest features — the nose and the front of the muzzle — are much closer to the camera than the ears, and project as disproportionately large. The face looks puffed out and bulbous. As focal length increases and I step back to compensate, that effect steadily relaxes: by 100mm (the "right way" shot, taken from much farther away and zoomed in), the camera is far enough away that the difference in distance between the nose and the ears is small relative to the total distance, so all features project at nearly the same relative scale. The face reads as flatter and more natural — much closer to how the toy actually looks in person.


13mm (close, wide-angle, distorted — "the wrong way") through 100mm (far, zoomed in, compressed — "the right way"), with the framing kept roughly constant across all five shots.

Part 2: Architectural Perspective Compression

For this part I flew a drone over a building with a large elliptical glass skylight set into a curved courtyard roof. The Start frame is a close, low pass directly over the skylight, filling the frame with the ellipse and its surrounding facade — depth cues like the curvature of the roofline and the ellipse's proportions are pronounced. The End frame is captured from much farther away and higher up, pulling back until the building is a small element embedded in the surrounding city block. As the camera-to-subject distance grows across the "increasing distance" sequence in between, the relative depth differences across the structure shrink: the skylight and roofline compress and flatten, and the building reads less as a 3D volume and more as a shape sitting on a map. That's the same underlying effect as the classic zoomed-in-from-far vs. walked-up-close comparison, just demonstrated by pulling a drone back through a whole sequence of distances rather than two discrete shots.


Start (close, low pass) → End (far, high pass), with four intermediate frames showing the perspective compress as distance increases.

Part 3: The Dolly Zoom

I recreated the dolly zoom with a drone (a Skyrover X-1) at night around campus, flown well clear of anyone and in otherwise empty spaces. For each take, I moved the drone backward while zooming in — or pushed in while zooming out — keeping the main subject roughly the same size in frame throughout, then stitched the stills/frames into a GIF. Because the subject stays a constant size while the center of projection moves, the background around it visibly swells or shrinks — the classic "Vertigo" effect. I shot three takes to compare how the effect reads against different subjects: the Campanile against the night skyline, a close pass along a building's curved glass facade, and a straight-down top view of the elliptical skylight from Part 2.
