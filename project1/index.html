One glass plate, three filtered exposures, and one reconstructed color photograph.

In 1907, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed scenes through blue, green, and red filters in sequence. The three exposures were recorded on a single glass plate. The Library of Congress later digitized these negatives, so each scan appears as a tall grayscale image containing three vertically stacked photographs.


A digitized plate, its three filter exposures, and the reconstructed color photograph.

Reconstructing a color image requires splitting each plate into its blue, green, and red exposures, then aligning the green and red channels to blue. I built the project in three stages:

  1. A brute-force displacement search for the small JPEG plates.
  2. A coarse-to-fine image pyramid for the larger TIFF plates.
  3. A gradient-based pyramid that matches structure rather than raw brightness.

The final pyramid reduces the largest search from roughly 130,000 full-resolution candidate shifts per channel to 1,086 local comparisons across the pyramid. More importantly, matching Sobel gradient magnitudes fixes the one plate that raw intensity matching cannot: emir. That failure turned out not to be a matter of choosing NCC over SSD, but of deciding what image information should be matched in the first place.

The final pipeline registers all 18 plates correctly.

01 · Brute-force alignment on small plates

I use the blue exposure as the fixed reference. For each green and red channel, I test every integer displacement \((\Delta y,\Delta x)\) inside a search window, score the alignment, and retain the best result.

A positive vertical displacement moves an image down; a positive horizontal displacement moves it right. This follows NumPy indexing, where the origin is at the upper-left corner.

Similarity is evaluated only over the central 80% of the image. This avoids two sources of error:

  • np.roll wraps pixels from one edge of the image to the other.
  • The plate boundaries are scratched, darkened, and inconsistent across channels.

I tested two similarity metrics.

Sum of squared differences

SSD is minimized:

\[ \mathrm{SSD}(I_1,I_2) = \sum_{p \in \Omega} \left(I_1(p)-I_2(p)\right)^2, \]

where \(\Omega\) is the cropped comparison region.

Normalized cross-correlation

NCC is maximized:

\[ \mathrm{NCC}(I_1,I_2) = \frac{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_1(p)-\mu_1\right)\left(I_2(p)-\mu_2\right)} {\sqrt{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_1(p)-\mu_1\right)^2}\; \sqrt{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_2(p)-\mu_2\right)^2}}. \]

The mean-centered form shows why NCC is useful. If two images differ by a global affine brightness transformation,

\[ I_2 = aI_1 + b, \]

the additive term \(b\) vanishes after mean centering, while the multiplicative term \(a\) cancels between the numerator and denominator. NCC is therefore insensitive to one global brightness scale and offset. Later, emir shows the limit of that assumption.

A numerical bug worth avoiding

Images must be converted to floating point before any arithmetic. With uint8, negative values wrap around:

\[ 0 - 1 = 255 \]

rather than

\[ 0 - 1 = -1. \]

This is especially harmful for SSD. A wrapped difference of 255 becomes

\[ 255^2 = 65{,}025, \]

while the correct squared difference should be

\[ (-1)^2 = 1. \]

Since SSD is minimized, unsigned overflow can make a poor alignment appear artificially favorable. This is not random numerical noise; it creates a systematic bias toward incorrect shifts.

02 · The image pyramid

The full-resolution TIFF plates require displacements as large as 178 pixels. A brute-force search with half-width \(d\) evaluates

\[ (2d+1)^2 \]

candidate shifts per channel. For \(d=180\), that becomes

\[ (2(180)+1)^2 = 130{,}321 \]

candidates. Each comparison operates on an image containing roughly 11.6 million pixels, so an exhaustive full-resolution search is too expensive.

The solution is a coarse-to-fine image pyramid. I first estimate the displacement on a small, downsampled image, then propagate that estimate upward while refining locally at each larger scale.

Let \(\mathbf{d}_{\ell}\) be the displacement at pyramid level \(\ell\), with \(\ell=0\) representing the full-resolution image. When each level is downsampled by two,

\[ \mathbf{d}_{\ell} = 2\mathbf{d}_{\ell+1} + \delta_{\ell}, \]

where \(\delta_{\ell}\) is a small local refinement at level \(\ell\).

The recursive algorithm is:

  1. If the shorter image dimension is below min_size, perform a brute-force search and return its displacement.
  2. Otherwise, Gaussian-blur and downsample both images by a factor of two.
  3. Estimate the lower-resolution displacement recursively.
  4. Double that displacement to return to the current resolution.
  5. Search a small window around the doubled estimate.

The multiplication in Step 4 is easy to omit. A displacement measured at half resolution must be doubled before it can be used at the next pyramid level. If the factor of two is missing, the final displacement is too small by

\[ 2^L, \]

where \(L\) is the number of pyramid levels.

On a two-level JPEG pyramid, that error is only a factor of four and can still look superficially plausible. On a five-level TIFF pyramid, it becomes a factor of 32: a true 178-pixel displacement can return as roughly 6 pixels.

Why blur before downsampling

Downsampling by two halves the Nyquist frequency. Frequencies above the new limit do not disappear when every other pixel is discarded; they alias into lower frequencies and create false structure.

Gaussian filtering removes those high-frequency components before decimation. The downsampled image then contains only structure that can be represented at the new resolution.

Computational cost

For a 3202-pixel plate repeatedly reduced until its shorter side reaches approximately 100 pixels, the pyramid evaluates 1,086 local shift candidates per channel instead of 130,321 full-resolution candidates. That is roughly 120 times fewer candidate evaluations, before considering that the early comparisons run on much smaller images.

Why NCC does not solve this case

NCC is invariant to a global relationship of the form

\[ I_2 = aI_1 + b. \]

That model does not describe emir. The robe changes relative brightness across channels differently from the rest of the image, so no single global \(a\) and \(b\) can map the blue exposure to the red one.

The problem is not a uniform brightness shift or contrast change. It is a spatially varying photometric change caused by the historical color filters.

Matching gradient magnitude

The final registration method matches Sobel gradient magnitude:

\[ M = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2}. \]

A Sobel response changes sign when contrast reverses, but gradient magnitude does not:

\[ (-G_x)^2 = G_x^2, \qquad (-G_y)^2 = G_y^2. \]

An edge therefore produces the same magnitude whether brightness rises or falls across it. The robe boundary remains visible in every channel despite its red-blue contrast reversal.

Gradient matching does not require corresponding pixels to have the same brightness. It requires them to preserve the same spatial structure.

The failure is also asymmetric: green aligns correctly under every tested method because its appearance still tracks blue. Only red fails because only red inverts the robe’s contrast. A geometric bug would be expected to affect both channels.

Icon: the coarsest level can be too coarse

Gradient matching fixes emir, but it initially broke icon, which had registered correctly with raw intensities.

With min_size = 64, the gradient-based pyramid returned

\[ (862, 128) \]

for the red channel instead of the expected result near

\[ (89, 23). \]

Icon contains repeated gilded ornament. At a 64-pixel base level, that ornament collapses into a nearly periodic texture. Several displacements score similarly, and the coarse search locks onto the wrong repeated feature. Higher-resolution refinement cannot escape once the pyramid begins in the wrong displacement basin.

Increasing the base-case size to 128 pixels preserves enough distinctive image structure to resolve the ambiguity.

05 · Enhancements

The final processing pipeline uses one fixed order and one parameter set for every plate:

\[ \text{gradient registration} \rightarrow \text{automatic crop} \rightarrow \text{gray-world white balance} \rightarrow \text{HSV saturation adjustment} \rightarrow \text{percentile contrast}. \]

Each before-and-after comparison changes only the stage being discussed. This makes the contribution of each enhancement easier to isolate.

1. Gradient-based registration

The registration method matches Sobel gradient magnitude rather than raw intensity. This discards edge polarity while preserving edge location and scene structure.

For emir, the red displacement changes from

\[ (416, -456) \]

to

\[ (107, 40). \]

The remaining plates move only slightly under gradient matching. Among plates that were already correctly aligned with raw intensity, the largest shift change is four pixels.

2. Automatic cropping

Even after registration, image borders remain unreliable. They contain dark plate edges, scratches, damaged regions, and wrapped image strips introduced by circular shifts. These regions create the green and magenta bands visible near the boundaries of several baseline results.

A fixed crop margin was not allowed, so I estimate the border automatically.

For each row or column, I combine two signals:

  1. The distance between its mean intensity and the image median.
  2. The disagreement among the three aligned channels.

A dark plate edge increases the first signal; a color fringe increases the second.

The threshold is based on the median and median absolute deviation of the interior region:

\[ \mathrm{MAD} = \mathrm{median}\left(|x_i - \mathrm{median}(x)|\right). \]

This lets the crop adapt to the noise level and border severity of each plate. The search is also limited to the outer 15% of each dimension, so a dark object or shadow inside the image cannot trigger a runaway crop.

A cropping bug

My first version selected the last row or column whose score exceeded the threshold anywhere inside the search window. A single dark band within the image could therefore drag the crop inward too far.

That version removed 29.9% of religious painting’s height and 29.9% of icon’s width.

The corrected method treats a border as a contiguous abnormal region beginning at the edge. It walks inward from each border and stops once the score settles. After that change, the crop removed 6.8% of religious painting’s height and 9.4% of icon’s width.

3. Automatic contrast

The contrast stage maps a lower percentile to 0 and an upper percentile to 1:

\[ I' = \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{I - p_{\mathrm{low}}}{p_{\mathrm{high}} - p_{\mathrm{low}}}, \; 0, \; 1\right). \]

I compute the percentiles jointly across all three channels rather than independently. This prevents contrast adjustment from functioning as an accidental white-balance operation.

Percentiles are more useful than raw minima and maxima because those values are determined by only one pixel each. A small number of scratches, clipped pixels, or digitization artifacts can occupy the full numerical range even when most of the image remains compressed.

For church, the cropped image already spans 0.004 to 1.000. A min-max stretch therefore changes almost nothing, despite the fact that most pixel values lie in a much narrower range.

Z-score normalization is identical here

I also implemented a z-score version of percentile contrast normalization, expecting it to behave differently. It did not: both versions produced pixel-identical output.

For

\[ z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}, \]

the percentiles transform as

\[ p_z = \frac{p_x - \mu}{\sigma}. \]

Substituting these values into the contrast-stretch expression causes \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) to cancel. Under this pipeline, percentile stretching before and after z-score normalization are mathematically equivalent.

4. Automatic white balance

Gray-world white balance assumes that the average scene color should be neutral. It rescales each channel so that all three channel means match:

\[ I_c' = I_c \frac{\bar{I}}{\mu_c}, \]

where \(\mu_c\) is the mean intensity of channel \(c\), and \(\bar{I}\) is the average of the three channel means.

Gray-world correction reduces the dominant color cast on many plates without requiring manual tuning.

I also tested white-world correction. Applied after percentile contrast stretching, it becomes an exact identity: contrast stretching ensures that every channel reaches 1, so all channel maxima are already equal by construction.

5. HSV color mapping

The Prokudin-Gorskii filters do not correspond exactly to modern sRGB primaries. Even a correctly aligned RGB stack can therefore appear muted.

To improve saturation without changing hue directly, I convert the image to HSV and rescale saturation toward a target value:

\[ S' = \mathrm{clip}\left(S \frac{S_{\mathrm{target}}}{P_{75}(S)}, \; 0, \; 1\right), \]

where \(P_{75}(S)\) is the image’s 75th-percentile saturation.

The clamp prevents already vivid images from becoming oversaturated and prevents low-saturation images from being stretched implausibly. Hue and value remain unchanged, so the method changes color strength rather than color identity.

This is not a true spectral correction. If the historical filters render a red object as slightly orange, saturation scaling will produce a more saturated orange rather than restoring the original red. A channel-mixing matrix could potentially adjust hue, but it would require calibration coefficients from a known color target, which these plates do not provide.

The saturation target and clamp limits were selected visually. This is the least principled part of the pipeline.

07 · Remaining limitations

A global translation is not always enough

Every method in this project applies one displacement vector,

\[ (\Delta y, \Delta x), \]

to the entire image.

That assumption fails when different objects move between the three sequential exposures. It can also fail when plate motion, optical distortion, or scene geometry produce local rather than global misalignment.

Three generations still shows faint color fringing along some high-contrast edges. In suna_river and harvesters, moving water or subjects create differences that no global translation can correct.

Potential extensions include per-region registration, local displacement fields such as optical flow, or searches over scale and rotation in addition to translation. I implemented none of these.

Cropping remains approximate

The automatic crop removes most major border artifacts, but it is not pixel-exact. Gradual border fading remains difficult because there is no strong transition for the detector to identify.

Gray-world white balance can be wrong

Gray-world assumes that the average scene color should be neutral. That is a poor assumption for scenes dominated by pale sky, grass, snow, stone, water, or a large colored object. The Gagarin grave plate makes this limitation especially visible.

Contrast has limited effect on some plates

The percentile stretch has little effect on the four additional Library of Congress scans because they already span most of the available numerical range.

  • Suna River: standard deviation changes from 0.270 to 0.274.
  • Locomotive: standard deviation remains 0.312.
  • Gagarin grave: standard deviation falls from 0.287 to 0.235.

This is the same outlier problem seen in the min-max comparison, but here it affects the input itself: a few extreme pixels can make an image appear to span the full range even when much of its meaningful content does not.

min_size = 128 comes from one failure case

I selected min_size = 128 because it corrected icon, then applied it globally to satisfy the fixed-parameter requirement. I did not sweep the parameter across the entire collection, so I cannot yet show whether 128 lies on a broad performance plateau or simply works well for this one plate.

Finally, “correct registration” is based on visual inspection. These historical plates do not provide ground-truth channel displacements.

08 · Implementation

The project uses NumPy throughout, with scikit-image used only for:

  • Image loading and saving
  • Gaussian filtering
  • Sobel filtering
  • RGB/HSV conversion

At load time, every plate is converted to floating point and split into three equal sections along the row axis. The channels appear top-to-bottom as blue, green, and red; they are not stored in RGB order.

Blue remains the fixed reference. Green and red are aligned independently to blue.

Similarity scoring uses a 10% interior crop. At full resolution, this margin is much larger than the local refinement window. At the coarsest pyramid level, however, the crop is only about 10–13 pixels wide, comparable to the search radius. Some extreme candidates at that level can therefore include a small number of wrapped pixels.

That is a real gap between the intended boundary handling and the current implementation.

Each function was tested against synthetic cases with known ground-truth shifts. All reported offsets, timings, tables, and output images come from one end-to-end run of the final pipeline.
