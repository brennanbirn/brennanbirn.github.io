Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Glass Plates

Brennan Birn · Fall 2026

One glass plate, three filtered exposures, and one reconstructed color photograph.

In 1907, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed scenes through blue, green, and red filters in sequence. Each result was recorded on a single glass plate. The Library of Congress later digitized these negatives, so each scan now appears as a tall grayscale image containing three vertically stacked photographs. Reconstructing the original color image means splitting the scan into its three channels and aligning the green and red exposures to the blue one.

I implemented three registration methods:

  1. A brute-force displacement search for the small JPEG plates.
  2. A coarse-to-fine image pyramid for the larger TIFF plates.
  3. A pyramid that matches Sobel gradient magnitudes rather than raw brightness.

The pyramid reduces the largest search from roughly 130,000 candidate shifts per channel to 1,086. Matching on gradients resolves the one plate that intensity-based alignment cannot: emir. The interesting part was not simply that raw NCC failed, but why it failed and why changing the similarity metric alone was not enough.

All 18 plates register correctly under visual inspection with the final pipeline.

01Brute-force alignment on small plates

I use blue as the fixed reference channel. For the green and red channels, I evaluate every integer displacement \((\Delta y, \Delta x)\) inside a search window, score the resulting alignment, and retain the best one.

A positive vertical displacement moves the image down. A positive horizontal displacement moves it right. This convention follows NumPy indexing, where the origin lies in the upper-left corner.

I score only the central 80% of the image. This avoids two problems:

  • np.roll wraps pixels from one side of the image to the other.
  • The plate boundaries are scratched, darkened, and inconsistent across the three exposures.

I tested two similarity metrics.

Sum of squared differences

SSD is minimized:

\[ \mathrm{SSD}(I_1,I_2)=\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_1(p)-I_2(p)\right)^2 \]

where \(\Omega\) is the cropped comparison region.

Normalized cross-correlation

NCC is maximized:

\[ \mathrm{NCC}(I_1,I_2)= \frac{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_1(p)-\mu_1\right)\left(I_2(p)-\mu_2\right)} {\sqrt{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_1(p)-\mu_1\right)^2}\; \sqrt{\sum_{p \in \Omega}\left(I_2(p)-\mu_2\right)^2}} \]

Writing NCC in mean-centered form makes its invariance clear. If one image is related to another through a global affine brightness transformation,

\[ I_2=aI_1+b, \]

then the additive term \(b\) disappears after mean centering, and the multiplicative term \(a\) cancels between the numerator and denominator. This matters later: NCC is invariant to one global affine intensity change, not to different transformations in different parts of the image.

On the three small JPEG plates, SSD and NCC returned identical offsets.

A numerical bug worth avoiding

Images must be converted to floating point before any arithmetic. With uint8, negative values wrap around:

\[ 0 - 1 = 255 \]

rather than \(-1\).

That is especially damaging for SSD. A wrapped difference of 255 becomes \(255^2\), while the correct difference should have been \(1^2\). Because SSD is minimized, this can make a poor alignment appear artificially favorable. It is not random noise; it systematically biases the search toward incorrect offsets.

02The image pyramid

The full-resolution TIFF plates require shifts as large as 178 pixels. A brute-force search over a displacement window with half-width \(d\) evaluates

\[ (2d+1)^2 \]

candidate shifts per channel.

For \(d=180\), that is

\[ (2(180)+1)^2 = 130{,}321 \]

candidates. On a plate containing roughly 11.6 million pixels per channel, evaluating that many full-resolution comparisons is not practical.

The solution is a coarse-to-fine pyramid: shrink the images, estimate a rough displacement at low resolution, then carry that estimate upward and refine it.

Let \(\mathbf{d}_\ell\) be the displacement at pyramid level \(\ell\), where \(\ell = 0\) is the original resolution. If each level is downsampled by a factor of two, then

\[ \mathbf{d}_{\ell} = 2\mathbf{d}_{\ell+1} + \delta_\ell, \]

where \(\delta_\ell\) is a small local correction found at level \(\ell\).

The recursive procedure is:

  1. If the shorter image dimension is below min_size, perform a brute-force search and return the result.
  2. Otherwise, blur and downsample both images by a factor of two.
  3. Recursively estimate the displacement at the lower-resolution level.
  4. Multiply the returned displacement by two.
  5. Search a small local window around that doubled estimate.

The factor of two in Step 4 is easy to omit and produces a quiet but severe bug. A displacement estimated on an image at half scale must be doubled before it can be used at the next level. If that factor is missing, the final estimate is too small by \(2^L\), where \(L\) is the number of pyramid levels.

For a two-level JPEG pyramid, the error is only a factor of four and can look plausibly close. For a five-level TIFF pyramid, the error is a factor of 32. A true 178-pixel shift can come back as roughly 6 pixels.

Why blur before downsampling

Downsampling by two halves the Nyquist frequency. Frequencies above the new limit do not disappear when every other pixel is discarded; they alias into lower frequencies and become false image structure.

Gaussian filtering removes those high-frequency components before decimation, so there is nothing left to fold into the lower-frequency image.

Cost

For a 3202-pixel plate whose shorter side is repeatedly halved until it reaches approximately 100 pixels, the pyramid evaluates 1,086 local shift candidates per channel rather than 130,321 full-resolution candidates. That is about 120 times fewer candidate evaluations before accounting for the fact that early levels operate on much smaller images.

03Where raw intensity fails

Emir: the metric is not the underlying problem

The emir plate fails dramatically in the red channel. Raw NCC returns

\[ (416,-456) \]

\[ (107,40). \]

The robe explains why.

The robe is therefore inverted relative to the rest of the scene. In blue, it appears bright. In red, it appears nearly black. A brightness-based matcher can score the true displacement worse than an unrelated image patch that happens to have a similar red intensity distribution.

My initial assumption was that NCC itself was the problem, so I tested SSD as well.

Why NCC does not save the plate

NCC is invariant to a global affine brightness relationship,

\[ I_2 = aI_1+b. \]

That assumption does not hold for emir. The robe transforms differently from the rest of the image. It has a different relative brightness ordering across color channels, so no single global \(a\) and \(b\) can describe the relationship between red and blue.

The problem is therefore not simply brightness scaling or brightness offset. It is a spatially varying photometric transformation.

Gradient magnitude fixes the relevant failure mode

Instead of matching raw intensities, the final method matches Sobel gradient magnitudes:

\[ M = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2}. \]

The Sobel responses themselves can change sign under contrast reversal. But the squared terms do not:

\[ (-G_x)^2 = G_x^2, \qquad (-G_y)^2 = G_y^2. \]

An edge produces a strong gradient magnitude whether brightness rises or falls across it. The robe boundary therefore remains visible in every channel despite the red-blue contrast reversal.

This is what the alignment algorithm needs: not identical brightness values, but consistent spatial structure.

The failure is also asymmetric. Green aligns correctly under every tested method because its appearance still tracks blue. Only red breaks, because only red reverses the robe's contrast. A geometric implementation error would be expected to affect both channels.

Icon: a coarser base level made alignment worse

Switching to gradient matching solved emir, but initially broke icon, which had aligned correctly with raw intensities.

With min_size = 64, gradient-based registration returned

\[ (862,128) \]

for the red channel instead of the expected result near

\[ (89,23). \]

The icon image contains repeated gilded ornament. At 64 pixels, that structure collapses into a nearly periodic texture. Several offsets become similarly plausible, and the coarsest search locks onto the wrong repeated feature. Once the pyramid commits to that basin, local refinement at higher resolutions cannot escape it.

Increasing the base-case size to 128 pixels preserves enough distinctive structure to disambiguate the repeated pattern:

Base-case sizeRed offsetResult
min_size = 64\((862,128)\)Wrong repeated feature
min_size = 128\((90,23)\)Correct

This is slightly counterintuitive: the coarsest pyramid level is not always the safest place to remove detail. It is where the algorithm chooses its global displacement basin, so it still needs enough unique visual information to make that decision.

05Enhancements

The final enhancement pipeline uses one fixed order and one parameter set for every plate:

\[ \text{gradients} \rightarrow \text{crop} \rightarrow \text{gray-world} \rightarrow \text{HSV color mapping} \rightarrow \text{contrast}. \]

Each before-and-after comparison below changes only the stage being discussed. This isolates the claimed effect.

1Better registration features

The registration method matches Sobel gradient magnitude rather than raw intensity. This removes edge polarity while retaining edge location and structure.

For emir, the red displacement changes from

\[ (416,-456) \]

to

\[ (107,40). \]

The remaining plates move only slightly under the gradient-based method. The largest shift change among plates that were already correct is four pixels.

The evidence appears in Figure 4 and Tables 3–4.

2Automatic cropping

Even after alignment, the borders are the least reliable parts of each image. They contain dark plate edges, scratches, damaged regions, and wrapped strips caused by circular shifts. Those regions create the magenta and green bands visible near image edges in Figures 1 and 2.

A fixed crop margin was not allowed, so I estimate the border automatically.

For each row \(r\), I combine two signals:

  1. The distance between the row's mean intensity and the image median.
  2. The degree of disagreement among the three aligned color channels.

A dark plate edge triggers the first term. A color fringe triggers the second.

The threshold is computed using the interior median and the median absolute deviation:

\[ \mathrm{MAD} = \mathrm{median}\left(|x_i-\mathrm{median}(x)|\right). \]

This lets the crop threshold adapt to the noise level of each plate.

The search is restricted to the outer 15% of each image dimension. An interior shadow or dark object therefore cannot cause the crop to continue indefinitely into the scene.

A cropping bug

My first implementation selected the last row whose score exceeded the threshold anywhere inside the search region. That behaves badly when an interior band or shadow happens to resemble a border: one false detection can pull the crop inward by a large amount.

It removed 29.9% of the height of religious painting and 29.9% of the width of icon.

The fix was to treat a border as a contiguous run originating at the edge. Rather than searching for the last anomalous row, the corrected method walks inward from the border and stops once the signal settles.

After that change, the crop removed 6.8% of religious painting's height and 9.4% of icon's width.

A limitation remains: borders that fade gradually do not produce a strong transition. A top band survives on harvesters and melons in Figure 12.

3Automatic contrast

Contrast stretching maps a lower percentile to 0 and an upper percentile to 1:

\[ I' = \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{I-p_{\mathrm{low}}}{p_{\mathrm{high}}-p_{\mathrm{low}}},0,1\right). \]

The percentiles are computed across all three channels jointly rather than independently. That prevents contrast adjustment from acting as an accidental white-balance operation.

Percentiles are preferable to the raw minimum and maximum because minima and maxima are single-pixel statistics. A few damaged or saturated pixels can occupy the full numerical range even when nearly all image content remains compressed.

For church, the raw intensity range is already 0.004 to 1.000. A min-max stretch therefore changes essentially nothing even though much of the image occupies a relatively narrow central band.

An unexpected identity: z-score normalization

I also implemented a z-score version of percentile contrast normalization, expecting it to produce a different result. It did not. The output was pixel-identical.

For

\[ z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \]

the percentile values transform in exactly the same affine way:

\[ p_z = \frac{p_x-\mu}{\sigma}. \]

Substituting these into the contrast-stretch expression causes \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) to cancel. The two approaches are mathematically equivalent under this pipeline.

That result initially looked like a plotting or implementation mistake, but the algebra shows that it is an identity.

4Automatic white balance

Gray-world white balance assumes that the mean scene color should be neutral. It scales each channel so that the channel means match:

\[ I'_c = I_c \frac{\bar{I}}{\mu_c}, \]

where \(\mu_c\) is the mean of channel \(c\), and \(\bar{I}\) is the mean across the three channel means.

Gray-world correction is effective on many plates. It reduces the dominant color cast without manually choosing a target color.

White-world correction, however, does nothing when applied after percentile contrast stretching. The reason is structural: contrast stretching clips each channel to include 1, so every channel maximum is already 1 by construction. White-world scaling based on channel maxima then becomes the identity.

Gray world also has a clear limitation: it fails when the scene average is genuinely not neutral. The Gagarin grave plate is the clearest example.

5Better color mapping

The Prokudin-Gorskii filters do not map cleanly to modern sRGB primaries. Even a correctly aligned RGB stack can therefore look muted.

To improve saturation without changing hue directly, I convert the image to HSV and rescale saturation toward a fixed target:

\[ S' = \mathrm{clip}\left(S \frac{S_{\mathrm{target}}}{P_{75}(S)},0,1\right), \]

where \(P_{75}(S)\) is the image's 75th-percentile saturation.

The clamp prevents already vivid plates from becoming oversaturated and prevents very low-saturation plates from being stretched implausibly. Hue and value remain unchanged, so this stage changes the strength of colors rather than their nominal color category.

This is not a true spectral correction. If the historical filters render a red object as slightly orange, saturation scaling will yield a more saturated orange rather than restoring the correct red. A channel-mixing matrix could adjust hue, but that would require calibration coefficients from a known color reference, which these plates do not provide.

The target saturation and clamp limits were chosen visually. This is the least principled component of the pipeline.

06All eighteen plates

Registration looks correct on all eighteen plates. The remaining visible defects are photometric or geometric rather than translational registration errors:

  • A top border survives on harvesters and melons.
  • Three generations retains faint color fringing at some high-contrast edges.
  • The Gagarin grave plate retains a green-magenta cast because gray-world white balance is poorly matched to the scene.

07Remaining limitations

The pipeline works well for translational registration, but several limitations remain.

A single global shift is not always sufficient

Every method here applies one displacement vector,

\[ (\Delta y,\Delta x), \]

to the entire image.

That assumption breaks when different parts of the scene move between the sequential exposures. It also breaks when optical effects, plate motion, or scene geometry produce local distortion.

Three generations still shows faint fringing around some high-contrast edges. On suna_river and harvesters, moving subjects create misalignment that cannot be fixed by one global translation.

Possible extensions include:

  • Per-region or piecewise registration.
  • Optical flow or local displacement fields.
  • Searches over scale and rotation in addition to translation.

I implemented none of these.

Cropping is approximate

The automatic crop finds major border artifacts, but it is not pixel-exact. Gradual border fading remains difficult because there is no sharp transition to detect.

Gray-world correction can be wrong

Gray-world white balance assumes the average scene color should be neutral. That assumption is poor for scenes dominated by pale sky, grass, stone, snow, or a large colored object. The Gagarin grave plate shows this failure most clearly.

Contrast does little on some plates

The contrast stretch barely activates on the four added Library of Congress plates because they already span most of the numerical range.

For example:

  • Suna River: standard deviation changes from 0.270 to 0.274.
  • Locomotive: standard deviation remains 0.312.
  • Gagarin grave: standard deviation drops from 0.287 to 0.235.

This is the same outlier issue seen in Section 5.3, but now it affects the input rather than the method: a few extreme pixels can make an image appear to occupy the full range even if the meaningful image content does not.

min_size = 128 was selected from one failure case

The choice of min_size = 128 fixes icon, and I applied it globally to satisfy the fixed-parameter requirement. However, I did not sweep this parameter across the whole data set to show that 128 lies on a stable performance plateau rather than simply working well for one plate.

Finally, “correct registration” is still based on visual inspection. These historical plates do not provide ground-truth channel displacements.

08Implementation

The implementation uses NumPy throughout, with scikit-image only for:

  • Image loading and saving.
  • Gaussian filtering.
  • Sobel filtering.
  • RGB/HSV conversion.

At load time, each plate is converted to floating point and split into three equal sections along the row axis. The channels appear top-to-bottom as blue, green, and red; they are not stored in RGB order.

Blue remains fixed as the reference channel. Green and red are aligned independently to blue.

Similarity scoring uses a 10% interior crop. At full resolution, that margin is much larger than the local refinement search radius. At the coarsest pyramid level, however, the crop is only about 10–13 pixels wide, comparable to the local search range. Some extreme candidates at that level can therefore include a small number of wrapped pixels.

That is a real gap between the intended boundary handling and the current implementation.

Each function was tested with synthetic images whose ground-truth shifts were known. All reported timings, offsets, and output images come from one end-to-end run of the final pipeline.
